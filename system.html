From a flipped switch to a truck on the road.

Five steps. Each one has the plain version first, and the engineering detail beside it if you want it.

Someone flips a switch.

Each switch is one need, and up means the shelter needs it. The dial says roughly how many people are there. That's the whole interface: nothing to charge, unlock or learn.

The detail

A mechanical switch bounces for a few thousandths of a second when it moves. The box waits until a switch has held still for 40 ms before it believes it, then chirps once so the person knows it noticed.

The dial is a knob with 11 clicks, read as a voltage. It has to rest on one click for 1.5 seconds before the box sends, so turning it past several clicks sends one report, not five.

Switch settle time40 ms
Dial settle time1.5 s
Dial positions0, 5, 10, 15, 20, 30, 40, 50, 60, 80, 100+
ControllerESP32-S3 on a Heltec V4.3

The people count is rough on purpose. Nobody counts heads exactly in an emergency, and "about 40" is what a responder needs.

The box writes a 31-byte report.

It doesn't stream anything. It writes one short line of text that says everything about the shelter right now, about the length of this sentence's first six words.

This is the exact text the box transmits. Pick any piece to see what it means.

The detail

Every report carries the whole state, not just what changed. If one is lost, the next one repairs the picture, and headquarters never has to remember history to make sense of a report.

The number after PKT goes up by one with each new report. Every copy of one report carries the same number, so headquarters can drop repeats and can tell when a report went missing altogether.

It's plain text, not packed bits. That costs a few bytes and means anyone can read a report on a serial monitor while debugging.

W M FD SH FUWater, medical, food, blankets, fuel: 1 or 0
OAbout how many people
HSeconds between check-ins. 0 means it only speaks when something changes

It crosses the hills by radio.

The radio is slow on purpose. Sending a few bytes slowly lets headquarters read a signal far too faint for a phone or a walkie-talkie, with no towers or repeaters in between.

The receiver can still read a signal about 150 dB weaker than what the box sends: a factor of a million billion.

Why SF9? Try the others.

The detail

This is LoRa, a radio method that spreads each bit over a long chirp. A higher spreading factor reaches further and takes longer on air. SF9 is the middle of the range.

Frequency915.0 MHz
Bandwidth125 kHz
Spreading factorSF9
Coding rate4/7
Time on air, one copy≈ 0.31 s Calculated
Decode limit≈ −129 dBm Calculated
Weakest decoded in the field−122 dBm Measured
Transmit powerAbout +22 dBm Estimated

The transmit figure is 12 dBm into the board's amplifier, which adds roughly 7 to 11 dB. It hasn't been measured with a meter.

Not legal to deploy in Japan as built. 915 MHz is a US band. A real deployment needs Japan's 920 MHz band, a certified radio module, and listen-before-talk in the firmware. I'm not a radio lawyer, so that needs checking against the actual rules.

Headquarters answers, and the box tells you.

Sent is not the same as received. The box sends each report up to three times and waits for headquarters to say "got it". Two quick beeps mean it arrived. One long beep means it's still trying.

    The detail

    The first range test lost 7.5% of single packets at the edge of range, in short bunches with no warning. Three copies cover the one-packet dropouts. For longer ones, the box waits for headquarters to confirm and retries until it does.

    Copies per report3, about 0.7 s apart
    Headquarters repliesAbout 80 ms after any copy
    Box listens after the last copy0.9 s
    If nothing comes backRetries after 5, 15 and 30 s, then every 60 s
    ConfirmedTwo quick beeps, light on for 1.5 s
    Not confirmedOne long beep, light double-blinks

    The copies are spaced with a little random jitter, so two boxes that start at the same moment don't keep colliding.

    Status. The confirm-and-retry firmware compiles and runs in simulation. It hasn't been tested on the real boards yet. Range test 2 is where that happens.

    Headquarters ranks who's first.

    When several shelters report at once, someone has to decide where the first truck goes. The dashboard adds up a small score for each shelter and shows its working, so nobody has to trust a black box.

    A score of 3 or more is high priority and rings an alarm until a person acknowledges it. A score of 2 is medium.

    If a box is set to check in on a timer, headquarters calls it late after 3 missed check-ins and lost after 10. A silent shelter is treated as a worry, not as good news.

    The dashboard is one web page that reads the receiver over USB. It needs no internet and no server.
