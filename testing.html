The first field test broke an assumption.

I assumed a report that was sent had arrived. 784 packets later, the data said otherwise, and the design changed twice because of it.

Range test 1: walk away until it stops.

October 18, 2026. Two Heltec V3 boards at full power. One stayed put and logged to a laptop; the other was carried away, sending a numbered packet every 2.2 seconds with no repeats. Each dot is one packet that arrived.

Signal strength of every packet received

dBm, over the 41 minutes of the test. Lower is weaker.

What changed because of it.

Every report is now sent three times. If losses are independent, the chance of losing all three copies is 0.043 × 0.043 × 0.043, about 1 in 12,500. That takes delivery from 95.7% to roughly 99.99% for three times the airtime.

Then headquarters started answering. Copies can't cross a seven-minute blackout. So the box now waits for a confirmation, and if none comes it retries after 5, 15 and 30 seconds, then every minute, until it gets through.

The box only talks when something changes. A packet every two seconds wasted battery and airtime to say nothing new.

What this test can't tell you. It didn't record distance, so there's no range figure here and I won't quote one. The "99.99%" is a prediction that assumes losses are independent. The confirm-and-retry firmware compiles and passes its simulated tests, and still needs time on real radios.

Range test 2 is next, and it has GPS.

The box will send a full three-copy report every five seconds while a phone riding with it records a GPS track. Afterwards a script joins every packet to a position.

That gives three things this page is missing: how far it reaches, how delivery falls off with distance, and whether three copies really behave the way the prediction says.
