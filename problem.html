The shelters that needed help most couldn't say so.

This project starts from one documented failure: after the 2024 Noto Peninsula earthquake, responders couldn't find out which cut-off community needed what.

Noto Peninsula, January 1, 2024.

A magnitude 7.6 earthquake hit a rural, mountainous peninsula on a public holiday, in winter. The roads in are few, and landslides closed them. What follows is what the record says happened next.

  • M 7.6

    earthquake in Ishikawa Prefecture. About 30,000 buildings were destroyed or badly damaged. The seafloor rose up to four metres in places, which put ports out of use.Nippon.com

  • 228 + 397

    people died: 228 directly, and 397 more from disaster-related causes in the months after. More people died in the aftermath than in the earthquake.Nippon.com, figures as of July 2025

  • 49

    locations were cut off, holding about 3,345 people. Some stayed cut off for more than a week, and rescuers reached some hamlets on foot through snow and mud.Ishikawa Prefecture tally; AFP, Al Jazeera and Malay Mail reporting, January 2024

  • 70%

    of mobile coverage was lost in the hardest-hit towns. More than 800 carrier base stations were down at the peak on January 3.Ministry of Internal Affairs and Communications; Cabinet Office

  • 31,800

    people were sheltering across the region at the peak.Contemporary reporting, January 2024

The first week

  1. Jan 1The earthquakeRoads, power and phones go down across the peninsula.
  2. Jan 3Phones at their worst800+ base stations are off the air.
  3. First 72 hoursThe window that mattersResponders can't confirm who is safe or where to send supplies.
  4. Jan 8Isolation peaksThe count of cut-off communities is at its highest a week in.
  5. About a week inSatellite internet arrivesUnits are airlifted to isolated parts of Wajima.

Help with connectivity did come. It came after the days when knowing where to go first would have mattered most.

Nobody could have pre-wired the right villages.

Ishikawa had done the homework. A 2013 survey listed 209 communities likely to be cut off in a disaster. When it happened, only 25 of the 49 places that were actually cut off were on that list. In Suzu and Noto Town, none of them were.

So a plan that protects only the predicted places misses about half. Whatever fills this gap has to be cheap enough to put everywhere, not only where the map says.

Japan already has disaster radio. It doesn't reach this far down.

It would be wrong to say no two-way system exists. Japan's municipal disaster radio has three parts, and one of them is two-way. The gap is narrower than that, and more specific.

  • 同報系Broadcast

    Town hall to residents, by loudspeaker.

    One way.

  • 移動系Field radio

    Headquarters to staffed shelters and vehicles.

    Two way, but it needs repeaters, and landslides and long power cuts took those out.

  • テレメーター系Telemetry

    River and rain sensors reporting automatically.

    Machines only.

  • The gapSmall, informal shelters

    A community hall, twenty homes, no trained operator.

    Nothing reaches here. Shelter Beacon is built for this.

The two-way field radio is provisioned for official, staffed places: municipal offices, large designated shelters, emergency vehicles. It needs trained people, dedicated equipment and working repeaters. Most of what was cut off in Noto was none of those.

The government names this gap itself.

The strongest evidence isn't outside commentary. It's Japan's own reviews and plans, which describe the problem in close to the same words.

The Noto self-inspection
The Cabinet Office's review of its own response lists "difficulty of situation assessment" as a core problem, and says better ways of gathering and sharing information about isolated communities should be pursued.Cabinet Office self-inspection report, June 2024
19,000 communities
That's how many farming and fishing communities the government counts as at risk of being cut off: about 800,000 households and 2.6 million people.Cabinet Office survey, fiscal 2013, the most recent national count
First on the list
The standing policy on isolated communities puts "securing information and communication in the initial phase" first of its four challenges. The 2023 National Resilience plan asks for the same.Cabinet Office; Cabinet Secretariat
The law after Noto
The revised law calls for stockpiles that account for small communities likely to become isolated.Nippon.com, January 2026

More supplies in a warehouse don't tell anyone where to send the first truck when several communities are cut off at once.That is the layer the reform leaves open, and the one this project works on.

The people affected are getting older.

Rural Japan holds about a fifth of the population on about 70% of the land, and it is ageing faster than the cities. A 2025 paper in the journal Disasters calls rural disaster resilience "somewhat under-researched" next to urban preparedness.

This is why the box has switches and a dial. Someone older, cold, and without training can use a switch. They shouldn't have to charge, unlock or learn anything.

  • ≈50%

    of residents in Wajima, Suzu, Noto Town and Anamizu were 65 or older in 2020. The national figure is about 30%.ICMA PM Magazine, July 2026

  • 20,000

    or so "marginal communities" across Japan where more than half the residents are over 65.Ministry of Internal Affairs data, cited in Watanabe, 2024

  • 65%

    of deaths in the 2011 Tōhoku earthquake and tsunami were people over 65.ScienceDirect, 2016

It will happen again, somewhere bigger.

Earthquakes in Japan aren't getting more frequent. The plates move at the rate they always have. What changed is how well the risk is understood.

In September 2025 the government's Earthquake Research Committee revised its estimate for a magnitude 8 to 9 Nankai Trough earthquake in the next 30 years to between 60% and 94.5% or higher. Officials were clear that this reflects better modelling, not a rising hazard. The last one was in 1946, and they come roughly every 90 to 150 years.

  • 60–94.5%

    chance of a Nankai Trough megaquake within 30 years.Earthquake Research Committee, reported by Japan Times, September 2025

  • 298,000

    deaths in the government's worst-case model, falling to about 90,000 if warning and evacuation work well.Cabinet Office, revised March 2025, via Nippon.com

  • 9.5 million

    people displaced in the same scenario.Reported by Fortune/AFP, January 2025

What else exists, and where each one stops.

Other people are working near this problem. None of these is a bad idea, and several would work well alongside the box. The search covered Japan's research agencies, the communications ministry and its regional bureaus, and the Digital Agency.

SystemWhat it doesWhere it stops for a small cut-off shelter
  • Satellite internetStarlink, deployed after Noto

    Full internet anywhere with a view of the sky. Now planned as permanent installs at 14 isolation-prone halls.

    It has to be delivered before it helps, so it can't tell anyone where to deliver it. It draws 50 to 75 W, and 14 halls is a small part of 19,000 communities.

  • Resilient mesh networkNICT NerveNet

    A government-funded network that keeps local connectivity alive and counts people in and out of official shelters.

    It restores a connection. It doesn't structure a needs report, and its rollout to isolated areas is still being planned.

  • Tablet check-in with supply mathsSeri, Takezaki and Ishida, 2025

    Face-recognition check-in at shelters. Headquarters works out supplies from live headcount with a fixed formula.

    It assumes a powered tablet, a camera and a network. It works out how much to send, not who is most urgent.

  • Phone-to-phone relayingHachijojima study, 2025

    Messages hop between phones as people move, studied for tsunami evacuation on a remote island.

    A routing simulation, not a deployed system. It moves messages and doesn't say what the message should be.

  • AI triage of patientsERTRIAGE

    A commercial app that triages individual patients from vital signs, offline, on an Android device.

    It ranks patients, not shelters, on a touchscreen, with a model nobody can check by hand, and it has no radio of its own.

  • Shelter BeaconThis project

    A switch box already on the wall, on its own battery and solar panel, that radios a shelter's needs straight to headquarters.

    It carries a few facts, not conversation. It tells responders where to go first. Satellite and mesh take over once they arrive.

Put plainly: this search found no project that combines long-range low-power radio, mechanical input and two-way needs reporting for small informal shelters. That is "none found", not "none exists".

So the box has to work like this.

No infrastructure
No cell network, no internet, no repeaters, no grid power. Radio straight to headquarters, on a battery and a small solar panel.
No training
Someone who has never seen it has to get it right, in the dark, with cold hands, on the worst day of their year.
Cheap enough to be everywhere
Half the places cut off in Noto weren't predicted, so it only helps if it's already on the wall. That means about a hundred dollars, not thousands.
Honest about itself
It has to tell the person whether headquarters actually heard them.

Where this research is thin.

It leans on English-language sources and reporting. The Japanese-language after-action reports from the Fire and Disaster Management Agency would strengthen it, and so would checking whether the communications ministry already runs low-power radio pilots aimed at this gap.

Some national figures are old: the count of 19,000 at-risk communities dates from fiscal 2013. And nobody in Japan's disaster services has reviewed this design yet. That conversation is worth more than any more reading.
