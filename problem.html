The shelters that needed help most couldn't say so.

This project starts from one documented failure: after the 2024 Noto Peninsula earthquake, responders couldn't find out which cut-off community needed what.

Noto Peninsula, January 1, 2024.

  • M 7.6

    earthquake in Ishikawa Prefecture. The seafloor rose up to four metres in places, which put ports out of use.Nippon.com

  • 228 + 397

    people died: 228 directly, and 397 more from disaster-related causes in the months after.Nippon.com, figures as of July 2025

  • 12+

    communities were cut off by landslides, some for more than a week. Rescuers reached some hamlets on foot through snow and mud.AFP, Al Jazeera and Malay Mail reporting, January 2024

  • 31,800

    people were sheltering across the region at the peak.Contemporary reporting, January 2024

  • 72 hours

    is the window that matters most for survival. Lost connectivity hampered confirming who was safe, coordinating rescue and delivering supplies inside it.World Economic Forum analysis

Two independent studies of the response reached the same conclusion: isolated evacuation centres had communication and supply problems, and medical teams were slow to reach remote shelters partly because nobody knew where the need was greatest.

Japan already has disaster radio. It doesn't reach this far down.

It would be wrong to say no two-way system exists. Japan's municipal disaster radio has three parts, and one of them is two-way. The gap is narrower than that, and more specific.

  • 同報系Broadcast

    Town hall to residents, by loudspeaker.

    One way.

  • 移動系Field radio

    Headquarters to staffed shelters and vehicles.

    Two way, but it needs repeaters, and landslides and long power cuts took those out.

  • テレメーター系Telemetry

    River and rain sensors reporting automatically.

    Machines only.

  • The gapSmall, informal shelters

    A community hall, twenty homes, no trained operator.

    Nothing reaches here. Shelter Beacon is built for this.

The two-way field radio is provisioned for official, staffed places: municipal offices, large designated shelters, emergency vehicles. It needs trained people, dedicated equipment and working repeaters. Most of what was cut off in Noto was none of those.

The government's fix is more supplies, not better information.

Japan's legal reforms after Noto name the problem directly. The revised law calls for stockpiles that account for small communities likely to become isolated.

That helps. But more supplies in a warehouse don't tell anyone where to send the first truck when several communities are cut off at once. Knowing which shelter needs what, right now, is the missing layer, and it's the one this project works on.

And the people affected are getting older.

  • ≈50%

    of residents in Wajima, Suzu, Noto Town and Anamizu were 65 or older in 2020. The national figure is about 30%.ICMA PM Magazine, July 2026

  • 20,000

    or so "marginal communities" across Japan where more than half the residents are over 65.Ministry of Internal Affairs data, cited in Watanabe, 2024

  • 65%

    of deaths in the 2011 Tōhoku earthquake and tsunami were people over 65.ScienceDirect, 2016

It will happen again, somewhere bigger.

Earthquakes in Japan aren't getting more frequent. The plates move at the rate they always have. What changed is how well the risk is understood.

In September 2025 the government's Earthquake Research Committee revised its estimate for a magnitude 8 to 9 Nankai Trough earthquake in the next 30 years to between 60% and 94.5% or higher. Officials were clear that this reflects better modelling, not a rising hazard. The last one was in 1946, and they come roughly every 90 to 150 years.

  • 60–94.5%

    chance of a Nankai Trough megaquake within 30 years.Earthquake Research Committee, reported by Japan Times, September 2025

  • 9.5 million

    people displaced in the government's worst-case model.Government scenario, reported by Fortune/AFP, January 2025

So the box has to work like this.

No infrastructure
No cell network, no internet, no repeaters, no grid power. Radio straight to headquarters, on a battery and a small solar panel.
No training
Someone who has never seen it has to get it right, in the dark, with cold hands, on the worst day of their year.
Cheap enough to be everywhere
It only helps if it's already on the wall of the community hall. That means about a hundred dollars, not thousands.
Honest about itself
It has to tell the person whether headquarters actually heard them.
